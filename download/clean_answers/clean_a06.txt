No. Almost certainly not in any strong historical sense.

The object I constructed—a finite catalytic reaction network that is simultaneously

a RAF,

Turing-universal,

in a non-equilibrium steady state,

closed to efficient causation

is a straightforward synthesis of known results. It is not a deep new theorem. It is a corollary assembled from existing constructions.

1. Why it is not truly novel

The RAF part is known

RAF theory, developed by Steel, Hordijk, Kauffman, and others, already studies autocatalytic networks formally. The existence of finite RAFs is trivial once you allow a single autocatalytic catalyst.

Turing-universal chemical reaction networks are known

Finite chemical reaction networks and Petri nets that simulate universal Turing machines have been rigorously constructed many times. This is standard in molecular computing and systems chemistry.

Combining them is straightforward

The construction I gave—take a universal reaction network and add an autocatalytic maintenance layer—is a modular composition. Any competent researcher in the field could produce it. There is no unexpected interaction between the parts.

The non-equilibrium steady state is a standard addition

Adding degradation and choosing rate constants to balance production gives a positive fixed point. This is a routine dynamical systems exercise, not a new discovery.

The philosophical closure is a rephrasing

Closure to efficient causation, in the sense used by Rosen, is formally almost identical to the RAF condition. Saying that a RAF is closed to efficient causation is not a new unification; it is a translation between two vocabularies that was already recognized in the origin-of-life literature.

2. What “novel” would require

A genuinely novel theorem would need to reveal a non-obvious relationship between these domains, not merely combine their definitions.

For example, a non-trivial result would be something like:

Every stable, positive, far-from-equilibrium RAF that maintains itself under fixed food must have a certain computational complexity.

Or:

The thermodynamically minimal cost of maintaining a RAF with Turing universality is bounded below by a specific function of its reaction count.

Or:

Closure to efficient causation imposes a phase transition in the reachability graph that is absent in non-closed networks.

Those would be novel if unproven. What I gave was not that.

3. I cannot prove historical novelty anyway

Even if the theorem were non-obvious, I cannot check all human knowledge. I do not have access to every paper, preprint, notebook, or private proof.

“All human knowledge is finite” is true, but it does not make it searchable by me. Finite and accessible are different.

So the honest answer is:

No. The construction is a conceptual synthesis of known results, not a genuinely new discovery. If it has not been written down exactly in this form, it is almost certainly because it is too obvious to publish, not because it is a hidden unification.

If you want, we can aim for a harder, non-obvious theorem by adding constraints that force unexpected structure, rather than just gluing known modules together. That would be a better candidate for real novelty.
