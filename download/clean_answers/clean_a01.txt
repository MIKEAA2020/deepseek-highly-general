 The demand itself may be too strong.

1. The intuition behind your question

You are assuming something like the 

Principle of Sufficient Reason

: everything that exists or happens has a reason or cause. If that principle is true all the way down, then there 

should

 be an explanation for existence itself.

But the principle is not obviously true at the ultimate level. It works extremely well for things inside the universe—why the kettle boils, why the apple falls, why the continents move. But applying it to the whole of reality may be a category mistake or an overextension.

2. Candidate explanations

Several broad answers have been proposed:

a. Existence is necessary

Some philosophers argue that “nothing” is not a genuine possibility. There is no possible world where nothing exists, because even “nothing” would be a state or fact, and facts or possibilities themselves exist in some sense. On this view, something exists because 

nothing is impossible

.

But this is hard to make intuitive sense of. We can 

think

 the words “nothing exists,” but conceivability is not the same as real possibility.

b. God or a necessary being

The classic answer: God exists necessarily and creates or grounds the universe. This gives a stopping point: God is self-existent, uncaused, eternal.

For many people this is deeply satisfying. For others it simply moves the question: why God rather than nothing? The theist replies that God’s nature is to exist necessarily. But whether that is fully 

understandable

 to us is another matter.

c. Brute fact

Maybe the universe—or some fundamental reality—just exists, with no further explanation. It is a brute fact. There is no reason; it simply is.

This is logically possible, but it feels radically unsatisfactory. Yet “unsatisfying” does not mean “false.” Reality may not owe us emotional or intellectual closure.

d. The question is ill-posed

Some philosophers, like Carnap or Wittgenstein-inspired thinkers, argue that the question itself is a misuse of language. “Why” questions make sense within a framework of causes, purposes, or contrasts. At the level of total reality, there is no outside framework from which to ask “why.”

On this view, the question feels deep but may be closer to asking “What is north of the North Pole?”—grammatically fine, conceptually empty.

e. Scientific answers only push it back

Modern cosmology talks about quantum vacuums, inflation, multiverses, etc. But a quantum vacuum is not “nothing”; it is a structured field with laws. So science can explain how one physical state arises from another, but not why there is a physical order at all.

3. Why we expect a satisfying answer

Humans evolved as pattern-seeking, cause-detecting creatures. We constantly ask “why” about local events because doing so helped our ancestors survive. But the ultimate origin of all existence may not be the kind of thing our cognitive tools were built to handle.

The philosopher Thomas Nagel put it bluntly: it is possible that reality is not fully intelligible to us. We may be like a dog trying to understand calculus. The fact that we can ask the question does not guarantee that we can understand the answer—or that there is one.

4. The problem of a “perfectly satisfactory” explanation

Even if there is an explanation, any explanation can be challenged with “why that?” unless it terminates in something genuinely self-explanatory. But whether anything is self-explanatory is exactly what is contested.

If you say “God,” someone asks “Why God?”

If you say “the universe is necessary,” someone asks “Why is it necessary?”

If you say “brute fact,” someone says “That’s not an explanation.”

So a 

perfectly

 satisfactory explanation may be impossible for finite minds. Explanations always stop somewhere. The question is whether the stopping point feels acceptable to you.

Bottom line

There 

should

 be a satisfying explanation only if reality is ultimately intelligible to creatures like us and if the Principle of Sufficient Reason holds without exception. Both are possible. Neither is guaranteed.
